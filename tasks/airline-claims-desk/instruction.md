Lakeshore Air's claims desk has a queue of passenger claims waiting to be settled. For every claim in that queue, the desk needs three answers: how much denied boarding compensation the passenger is owed, how much money must be refunded, and the date by which that refund has to be paid. Decide each claim under 14 CFR Part 250 and Part 260 as they were in force on October 1, 2026. No other law, guidance or policy applies, and Lakeshore's contract of carriage does not give passengers anything beyond what those two parts require.

All of the input data is in /app/data/. The file /app/data/claims.json contains one record per claim. Each record states the claim type and describes the ticket, including how it was paid for, whether it is an award ticket bought with miles, the cabin, and the list of coupons, where each coupon carries its flight, its times and its fare. A record also lists the paid extras, giving for each one its fee, the coupon it belongs to, and whether it was actually provided. It lists the checked bags, giving for each one its fee and whether that fee is linked to that specific bag by a unique identifier. Finally, each record holds the incident details, including when Lakeshore notified the passenger of a cancellation or change and when the passenger answered, and the date on which the passenger asked for a refund, if they did. For each connection, the ticket also states whether the checked bags were tagged through and whether the passenger left the airport. The file /app/data/flights.json describes every flight, with its times, its aircraft, its designed seat count, its scheduled service, and the lowest cash fare offered in each cabin. The file /app/data/airports.json gives the city, country and time zone of each airport, /app/data/carrier.json gives the carrier's details, and /app/data/federal_holidays.json lists the observed federal holidays you should use.

Lakeshore Air is a U.S. air carrier that operates scheduled passenger service. It is the merchant of record for every ticket and every fee in the queue, and no ticket agent is involved in any of the sales. Every itinerary was bought as one ticket in a single transaction, and Lakeshore operates every flight in the data. Every amount of money in the data is expressed in cents. Flight times, which include the times on each coupon, the new times of a changed flight and the planned arrival of replacement transportation, are local times at the airport concerned, as an airline publishes them. Every other timestamp in the data ends in Z and is in UTC. The fare shown on a coupon already includes all mandatory taxes and fees, but it does not include any paid extras. When a passenger was denied boarding, the fare to the destination or first stopover is the sum of the coupon fares starting with the denied flight and running up to that point. A trip is international, which is to say foreign air transportation, when any of its coupons touches an airport outside the United States, and every other trip is domestic.

Where a record describes replacement transportation, the planned arrival it gives is the arrival that was planned at the moment the replacement was arranged, and it refers to the airport that matters for that particular claim. None of the passengers in the queue is an individual with a disability. A passenger who declines a change that is not significant has chosen not to travel, so an extra booked for that trip that went unused was not provided because of the passenger's own choice. A passenger who turns down or does not answer the alternatives offered for a cancelled or significantly changed flight uses no part of that ticket. A flight counts as cancelled from the moment Lakeshore notifies the passenger, and a changed flight departs at its new scheduled time. Every date, including the day on which a flight departs, is a calendar date in UTC, and the day from which a refund due date is counted is not itself included in the count. The records are complete and correct.
